Burn severity mapping compares the Normalized Burn Ratio before and after the fire. Here you get the dNBR formula, the USGS class thresholds you will apply in the lab, and the checklist for choosing the pre- and post-fire images – the step where most reports go wrong.

Useful spectral indices

The Awesome Spectral Indices repository lists hundreds of indices with band formulas. For fire mapping the two relevant ones are NBR (Normalized Burn Ratio) and NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index).

dNBR

dNBR = NBR_pre − NBR_post, by convention often scaled × 1000. Positive values mean loss of vegetation; negative values mean regrowth.

Caveats

The thresholds were derived for US forests; in Mediterranean shrub or Polish pine they shift – calibrate with field data or at least report them as "USGS proposed". RdNBR = dNBR / √|NBR_pre| normalises for the pre-fire vegetation amount and is fairer when comparing forest with grassland.

Choosing the pre- and post-fire images

  • Pre: as close as possible before ignition, same season, cloud-free; a median of 2–4 weeks works.
  • Post (initial assessment): 1–4 weeks after containment – smoke gone, ash still there. An extended assessment uses the next growing season with the same phenology as pre and measures mortality, not char.
  • Same sensor, same processing level (L2A, COPERNICUS/S2_SR_HARMONIZED), same band set; never mix Landsat pre with Sentinel post without harmonisation.
  • Mask clouds per pixel (SCL or QA60) – a cloud in pre looks like high severity.

Checklist before you trust the map

1. FIRMS timeline: when did the fire start and end? 2. Pre and post composites in true colour: clouds, snow, harvested fields? 3. dNBR histogram over the perimeter: bimodal, unburned peak near 0? 4. The "unburned" class outside the perimeter: if it shows low severity everywhere, your dates differ in phenology. 5. Area per class in hectares – the number for the report.
