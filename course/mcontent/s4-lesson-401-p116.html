ICEYE and commercial SAR constellations

ICEYE, a Finnish company (Espoo) with a large office in Warsaw, builds small X-band SAR satellites and sells access to the data of the largest SAR constellation – more than 70 satellites launched by 2026. This allows near-real-time monitoring of surface changes in all weather conditions with a spatial resolution down to 25 cm and a revisit time of a few hours. ICEYE sells ready flood extent and depth products to insurers and governments; they were used in the Polish flood response of September 2024. Other commercial constellations: Capella and Umbra (USA), TerraSAR-X / PAZ (Germany / Spain), COSMO-SkyMed (Italy). In major disasters all agencies task their satellites for free through the International Charter "Space and Major Disasters".

Step by step: the UN-SPIDER flood script

  1. Compare optical and radar in the Copernicus Browser. Open Sentinel-2 and Sentinel-1 of the same days over your event and see what the clouds do to the optical image.
  2. Open script 2 and save a copy in your repository. Draw or import your AOI as geometry.
  3. Set the parameters at the top of the script: before-period and after-period dates (about 2–3 weeks each, the after-period starting at the flood peak), polarisation VH, orbit pass DESCENDING (or ascending – check which one covers your area on the right days), difference threshold 1.25.
  4. Run it and inspect the layers: before, after, difference, flooded area. The script computes the change like this: 
    // UN-SPIDER: ratio of the two dB images, flood where the ratio exceeds 1.25
var ratio = afterF.divide(beforeF);           // both images are in dB!
var flooded = ratio.gt(1.25);                 // after value at least 25 % more negative
// cleaner equivalent: the difference in dB
// var flooded = afterF.subtract(beforeF).lt(-3);   // at least 3 dB darker
    The script divides the dB values, so a ratio of 1.25 means the after value is at least 25 % more negative (e.g. −12 → −15 dB, roughly 3 dB darker for a typical land pixel); it is not a physical ratio of backscatter. The difference in dB does the same more transparently.
  5. Understand the masks. Permanent water (JRC), slopes steeper than 5° (radar shadow) and isolated specks (fewer than 8 connected pixels) are removed: 
    var jrc = ee.Image('JRC/GSW1_4/GlobalSurfaceWater').select('seasonality');
var permanent = jrc.gte(10);                   // water 10 or more months a year
var dem = ee.Image('WWF/HydroSHEDS/03VFDEM');
var slope = ee.Terrain.slope(dem);
flooded = flooded.updateMask(permanent.not())
                 .updateMask(slope.lt(5))
                 .updateMask(flooded.connectedPixelCount(25).gte(8)); // remove specks
  6. Read the flooded area (and, in the script, the exposed population and cropland): 
    var area_ha = flooded.multiply(ee.Image.pixelArea()).divide(1e4)
  .reduceRegion({reducer: ee.Reducer.sum(), geometry: roi, scale: 10, maxPixels: 1e10});
print('flooded area [ha]', area_ha);
  7. Sensitivity test. Change the threshold to 1.15 and 1.40 and the speckle filter radius (e.g. 30 m and 100 m); write down the flooded area for each run in a small table – this is the uncertainty of your map.
  8. Validate. Compare your map with the Global Flood Monitoring product (global-flood.emergency.copernicus.eu) or a Copernicus EMS map of the same event; write one paragraph of agreement and disagreement and say what you compared against.
  9. Optional: run script 1 (optical, Sentinel-2) for the same event and compare the two areas.

You are done when…

…the flood layer lies along the river and not on mountain slopes, the permanent lakes are not counted, and the area changes by less than a factor of two between the thresholds 1.15 and 1.40.
