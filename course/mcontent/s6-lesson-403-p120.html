Why does a burned forest look different to a satellite? Healthy vegetation reflects strongly in the near infrared (NIR) and weakly in the short-wave infrared (SWIR); after a fire the pattern reverses. This page shows the two reflectance curves that the Normalized Burn Ratio is built on.

From the curves to an index

The Normalized Burn Ratio uses exactly the two bands that move in opposite directions:

NBR = (NIR − SWIR2) / (NIR + SWIR2) = (B8 − B12) / (B8 + B12) for Sentinel-2, (SR_B5 − SR_B7) / (SR_B5 + SR_B7) for Landsat 8/9. The lab script uses B8 (10 m, broad NIR); the UN-SPIDER "In detail" page uses B8A (20 m, narrow NIR), which is the closer analogue of Landsat B5 and matches the 20 m B12 without resampling – both are acceptable, state which one you used.

  • NBR is high for green forest (0.3–0.7), near zero for bare soil and strongly negative for freshly burned areas (−0.2 … −0.5).
  • Why NBR and not NDVI? NDVI saturates in dense forest and recovers quickly with grass; the SWIR2 band (2.2 µm) reacts to char and soil moisture and stays changed for months. NDVI difference is still a useful second opinion in grassland.

Resolution

B12 (SWIR2) is a 20 m band on Sentinel-2, so the NBR result is effectively 20 m, not 10 m. Export at 20 m.
