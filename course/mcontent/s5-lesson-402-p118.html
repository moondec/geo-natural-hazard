Lab 5 (14.12, 3 h): you run the landslide density heatmap script of Handwerger et al. (2022) on a real event, tune its parameters and judge where it works. At the end you have an exported heatmap GeoTIFF and a short note – milestone M5, due at the start of lab 6 (21.12).

Before you start

  • The method is described in Handwerger et al. (2022), NHESS 22, 753–773. It uses Sentinel-1 Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) amplitude in the VH polarisation only: a stack of images before and after the event, same orbit, each reduced to its temporal median (pixels ≤ −30 dB removed); the pixel-wise log-ratio of the two medians; masks for flat terrain (slope < 5° in Hiroshima, < 10° at the other sites), curvature and permanent water (NASADEM) – no built-up mask; the pixels above the 99th percentile of the AOI are flagged as potential landslides and turned into a kernel density heatmap (100 m cells, 1–3 km radius). No training data are needed.
  • The script: GEO2 › 05_landslides › 03_full_workflow_hokkaido_2018 (steps 1–2 separately: 01, 02). The link opens only when you are signed in to an Earth Engine account with a registered Cloud project (lab 1).
  • Public alternative: the authors' GitHub repository for Handwerger et al. (2022) (listed in the article's "Code and data availability" section) holds 30 scripts that reproduce the figures of the paper, each with its own Code Editor link in the README. Open Figure 6a (Hokkaido 2018 earthquake heatmap) or Figure 4a (Hiroshima 2018, 99th-percentile threshold) and copy it into a new Code Editor file.
  • Formula you will inspect: I_ratio = 10 · log10( I_pre / I_post ) = I_pre − I_post in dB. A positive value means the backscatter decreased after the event – vegetation removed, fresh scar – which is where landslides appear; there is no absolute value.

Steps

  1. Choose an event with a known date and a reference inventory: one of the paper's cases (Hokkaido earthquake 6.09.2018, Hiroshima rainfall July 2018, Haiti earthquake 14.08.2021) or a landslide activation of Copernicus EMS.
  2. Open the script and set the area of interest (draw a geometry or import an asset), the event date, the pre-event window (months) and the post-event window (weeks), and the Sentinel-1 orbit (ascending or descending – do not mix). Run it and note how long the computation takes.
  3. Inspect the I_ratio layer (VH) with the Inspector tab: click a known landslide (positive, several dB) and an untouched slope (near zero) and compare the values.
  4. Change the threshold (the 99th percentile; try the 90th and 80th as in the paper's Fig. 4), the kernel radius (1–3 km) and the length of the pre-event stack (longer = less speckle noise). Re-run and watch how the heatmap changes.
  5. Overlay a reference: the EMS grading map (upload the shapefile as an asset) or a Sentinel-2 true-colour image of the first clear day after the event. Judge the visual agreement.
  6. Export the heatmap with Export.image.toDrive (set scale and region), start the task in the Tasks tab and download the GeoTIFF.
  7. Write a short note: event, parameters, where the method works and where it fails (radar shadow and layover? floods? agriculture or logging?).

Deliverable – milestone M5 (due 21.12, at the start of lab 6)

A landslide heatmap note: the parameters you used (AOI, event date, pre/post windows, orbit, percentile threshold, kernel radius), one figure of the heatmap with the reference overlaid, and the link to your saved script.

You are done when

the heatmap highlights the valleys that the reference inventory shows as hit, the I_ratio on untouched slopes stays near zero, flat valley floors are masked out, and the GeoTIFF opens in QGIS with the right coordinates.

Common problems

Empty image collection in the Console – the pre or post window contains no Sentinel-1 scene of the chosen orbit; widen the window or switch orbit. A blocky heatmap everywhere – the threshold is too low or the pre-event stack is too short. Flat areas flagged as landslides – floods or crop changes; keep the slope mask on.
