What is a landslide?

A landslide is the downslope movement of soil, rock and organic material under the effect of gravity, and also the landform that results from such movement (USGS). From orbit you see the scar (bare, bright, steep source area), the track and the deposit (fan, dammed river). Sizes range from about 10 m (one Sentinel pixel) to square kilometres; most rain-triggered landslides are small, so resolution matters more than for floods.

Basic landslide types

Landslides are classified by the type of material and the type of movement (Varnes classification, updated by Hungr et al. 2014). The material is either rock or soil (or both); soil is called earth if mainly sand-sized or finer particles and debris if composed of coarser fragments. The movement describes the internal mechanics of how the mass is displaced:

  • Fall and topple – rock detaching from cliffs.
  • Slide – rotational (slump) or translational (planar) movement along a surface.
  • Flow – debris flow, mudflow, earthflow; fast, with a long run-out.
  • Spread and complex or composite movements.

Causes vs. triggers

  • Preconditions (why here): slope angle and aspect, lithology (clay, flysch, weathered rock), soil depth, drainage, land cover, old landslides, road cuts, deforestation. These are the predictors of a susceptibility model.
  • Triggers (why now): intense or prolonged rainfall (the global majority), earthquakes (thousands of co-seismic landslides at once), snowmelt, river undercutting, volcanic activity, human loading or excavation.

Why this matters for the satellite method

A rain- or earthquake-triggered event gives a sharp before/after date, so change detection works – this is what you do in the lab. Slow creep over years has no event date and needs InSAR time series instead (see lecture 5).
